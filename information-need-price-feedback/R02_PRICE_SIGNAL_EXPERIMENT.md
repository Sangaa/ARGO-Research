# R02 — Candidate price-signal experiment and falsification plan

**27 November 2026 · SESSION-DERIVED · NON-CANONICAL · PROPOSAL ONLY**

## Candidate question

In a fictional, controlled market, does showing new participants a price trace generated by a separate group reacting to the same unverified rumor change their perceived information sufficiency or independent verification behavior relative to a matched fixed-price condition, when no new fundamental evidence is supplied?

This is not yet a claim of a research gap. It may prove to be a replication or a known combination. The next literature review must compare this exact sequence with price-cascade, social-learning, disclosure, rumor and RISP experiments.

## Causal units that must stay separate

| Node | Operational meaning | Example measure |
|---|---|---|
| Event | Whether the fictional fundamental event occurs | Preregistered hidden state |
| Signal | Rumor, source, time, repetition and later clarification | Exact text and provenance |
| Belief | Participant’s probability judgment and confidence | Probability estimate before/after |
| Information need | Perceived current knowledge and desired sufficiency threshold | Separately adapted RISP measures |
| Action | Whether the participant seeks the independent source, avoids it, or trades in a simulation | Observed choice, separated by action |
| Outcome | Price or network outcome after actions | Kept distinct from belief and need |

The price is informative about prior participants’ actions and may be treated rationally as a social signal. We do not call it “no information.” The manipulation tests whether that social signal changes subsequent sufficiency judgments or verification when no new fundamental document is added.

## Candidate design

1. Use a fictional scenario with a truth state fixed before data collection; no real money or real rumor.
2. A separate generation group sees only the rumor and trades in a simulated market. They receive no private signal about the event. Preserve the price path and its generation record.
3. Randomly assign a new participant sample to see either the generated price path with its social origin disclosed, or a prespecified fixed-price control. No participant in the measurement sample affects the displayed path.
4. Keep rumor wording, source, fundamental evidence access, timing and task identical across conditions.
5. Measure probability belief, confidence, current knowledge, sufficiency threshold, verification choice, avoidance and simulated trading as separate outcomes. Do not infer mediation from correlation.
6. Predefine one primary outcome, a smallest practically meaningful difference, sample size logic, exclusions, model and stopping rule before data collection.

## Falsification and interpretation

The narrow hypothesis is that the generated price trace changes the new participant’s sufficiency gap or verified-source choice relative to fixed price. If uncertainty intervals exclude the predefined meaningful effect, the hypothesis fails for this scenario. If the price changes belief but not sufficiency or verification, report that pattern without relabeling it as a need effect. If participants interpret the trace as evidence about the event’s fundamentals despite the disclosed origin, document the manipulation failure and do not claim clean isolation.

## Design threats

- Participants may update rationally from others’ trades; this is part of the social-information channel, not a nuisance to erase.
- The generation group may encode private information accidentally; its information set must be controlled.
- A fixed price may differ in salience or realism. A stronger control may be required after reviewing closest experiments.
- Adapted RISP measures require validity and comprehension checks for the financial context.
- Between-person interference is prevented by using separate generation and measurement groups.
- Price and information-seeking decisions can feed back on each other; the measurement sample cannot alter the displayed signal.
