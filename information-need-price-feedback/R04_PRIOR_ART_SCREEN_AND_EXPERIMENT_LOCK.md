# R04 — Prior-art screen and experiment boundary
**Status:** screening memo; candidate unresolved; no claim of novelty.
**Lane:** information need / rumor-linked price signals.
**Session attribution:** HORUS contributed as an internal analytical-agent review in this work. No separate HORUS conversation/session identifier was created or verified.

## Question under review
In a controlled, non-investment decision task, does seeing a price trace generated by a separate group reacting to the same unverified rumor change a participant's *perceived information-sufficiency gap* and independently verified information-seeking, relative to seeing the rumor without that trace?

This is narrower than asking whether rumors move prices or whether social signals affect decisions. The price itself may be informative about other participants' actions, so it must not be described as “no added information.”

## Prior-art screen (targeted, not systematic)
1. **Information sufficiency and seeking are established constructs.** Griffin, Dunwoody & Neuwirth's RISP model includes information sufficiency, perceived learning capacity, channel beliefs, and information seeking/processing. Kahlor's study measures the perceived gap between current understanding and what is needed to decide. Therefore neither “need for information” nor a knowledge/sufficiency gap is new by itself.
2. **Rumor/social-media effects on prices and price discovery are established.** Wu et al. study social-media activity around rumor clarifications and report changes in short- and longer-term price discovery. Merger-rumor studies also examine social media as a channel that can distort price discovery. Therefore “rumor affects price” and “social activity affects price discovery” are not new claims.
3. **Prices can carry social information and create cascades.** Experimental asset-market research studies informational price cascades and non-aggregation of asymmetric information. Sequential search models also examine how price information and word-of-mouth change information acquisition. Therefore a price trace cannot be treated as a neutral visual cue.
4. **Verification is already a studied behavior.** Merlino, Pin & Tabasso model costly verification during rumor diffusion; experiments on social presence also test fact-checking behavior. The dependent variable “verification” alone is not novel.

### Screening conclusion
The broad candidate from R06 (“price feedback changes information seeking”) is too broad to support an originality claim. The narrower joint combination—manipulating the *known origin and informational content* of a rumor-linked price trace, while measuring both perceived sufficiency gap and independent verification in the same participant-level experiment—remains **unresolved**, not demonstrated as novel. This is an initial targeted screen, not a reproducible systematic review. Search terms, databases, date coverage, and inclusion/exclusion criteria still need a logged review.

## Experiment boundary (design proposal, not executed)
- Use fictional assets and fictional claims, with no real-money decisions or real-world rumors.
- Randomly assign participants to: (A) rumor only; (B) rumor plus trace from a separate group exposed to the same rumor and explicitly known to have no extra private evidence; (C) rumor plus a matched fixed/neutral trace. A further arm can vary whether the trace's source and information set are disclosed.
- Keep the underlying payoff distribution and all factual evidence identical across arms. Do not imply the trace adds no information; disclose that it is endogenous behavior and measure participants' beliefs about what it reveals.
- Before and after exposure, measure perceived current knowledge, the participant's sufficiency threshold for the decision, and their estimated gap. Then provide an equally available verification source; measure whether they seek it, latency, and whether they use it.
- Primary outcomes: change in perceived gap and verified information-seeking. Secondary outcomes: belief calibration, confidence, and decision accuracy. Pre-register the primary contrast and exclusions before collecting any data.
- A result where the price trace changes verification is not automatically evidence of a general “need propagation” mechanism. Competing explanations include rational social learning, conformity, anchoring, trust in the market, and attention.

## Evidence limits and next gate
- No experiment has been run and no participant or market data were collected.
- The literature screen above is selective and cannot establish priority or absence of prior work.
- Next research gate: perform and log a systematic search across communication, behavioral economics, finance, and misinformation research; retrieve full texts for the closest studies; build an overlap table; revise or abandon the question if a close prior design exists.
- Preserve the negative result: the component ideas are established and the original broad framing is not supportable as novel on this screen.

## Experience / error learning candidate
**Failure:** treating a price movement generated by a rumor-reacting crowd as “no new information.”  
**Correction:** the price can reveal actions and may rationally update beliefs about other participants' signals. A future experiment must disclose and manipulate the trace's origin/information set, and separately measure inferred social information, sufficiency gap, and verification.  
**Status:** session-derived, non-canonical; requires independent review and protocol promotion.
